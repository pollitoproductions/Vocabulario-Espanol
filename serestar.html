Ser vs. estar, in short

Ser and estar both mean “to be,” but they split the job between them, and choosing the wrong one can change what a sentence means. This tool drills exactly that choice: 100 sentences, five subjects, present tense only. For the full explanation — with the DOCTOR and PLACE memory tricks and the stories behind them — read the guide: Ser vs. estar.

Ser is for what something is, in a lasting way: identity (Ella es profesora), profession, origin (Él es de Perú), physical traits and character (alto, simpático), material (de madera), possession (el perro es de Luis), and time (Es la una, Son las tres) — including when events happen (La película es a las tres).

Estar is for how something is right now, or where it is: location (en casa, en la escuela), condition (enfermo, cansado, dormido), emotion (feliz, triste, nervioso), what someone is doing right now (está comiendo), resulting states (la puerta está cerrada), being ready (listo para salir), and position (de pie).

Tricky pairs: five adjectives change meaning with the verb, and this tool gives you both sides of each: aburrido — ser: boring / estar: bored · bueno — ser: kind / estar: tastes good · listo — ser: smart / estar: ready · malo — ser: mean / estar: sick · rico — ser: rich / estar: tastes good. In those questions the context is the clue — bored in class, ready to go out, sick at home. Read the situation, not just the adjective.

Ser: identity · profession · origin · traits · material · possession · time

Estar: location · condition · emotion · progressive · resulting state · ready · position
